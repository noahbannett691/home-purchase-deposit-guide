How Deposits Are Held in Trust During a Home Purchase

When an offer on a home is accepted, the deposit is usually the first serious money that changes hands, and many buyers are surprised by how quickly it must be delivered. In Ontario the deposit is typically due within 24 hours of acceptance, and it is not paid to the seller directly. It is held in a trust account until closing, which is why working with a Hamilton Real Estate Lawyer at Estofa Law from the moment your offer is accepted keeps that money protected and every deadline around it under control.

What a trust account actually is

A trust account is a separate bank account that a law firm or a real estate brokerage maintains only for client money. Funds in trust do not belong to the firm, cannot be used for its own expenses, and are tracked client by client. When your deposit is delivered, it goes into the trust account named in your agreement of purchase and sale, and it stays there until the transaction closes or the deal falls apart under the terms of the contract.

Who holds the deposit and why it matters

The agreement states whose trust account receives the deposit, either the listing brokerage or a law firm. Either way, the holder is a trustee for that money. It cannot simply hand the deposit to the seller because the buyer paid it. Release before closing requires written direction signed by both buyer and seller, or a court order. This single rule prevents most deposit disputes, because neither side can pressure the holder into releasing funds early.

Deadlines and the form of payment

Missing the deposit deadline is a breach of the agreement, even if the delay is only a day, and a breach can give the seller the right to walk away and keep the deposit. Deposits are normally delivered by bank draft or certified cheque, and increasingly by wire transfer where the receiving office accepts it. Keep proof of delivery, confirm the exact name the draft must be made out to, and never make a deposit draft payable to the seller personally unless your agreement specifically says so.

What happens to the deposit on closing day

On closing, the deposit is credited toward your purchase price. If you paid a deposit of 50,000 dollars on a 700,000 dollar home, you bring the remaining balance, adjusted for taxes, utilities and other statement items, and the deposit forms part of the total the seller receives. Your lawyer's statement of adjustments shows exactly how the deposit was applied, so there is a paper trail from the day the money entered trust to the day it was released.

If the deal does not close

If a transaction fails, the deposit does not automatically go to either party. Where both sides agree on what should happen, they sign a mutual release and the trustee follows it. Where they disagree, the money stays in trust until the dispute is settled or a court decides. This is frustrating in the moment, but it is far better than chasing money that was already spent. A careful review of conditions and deadlines before they expire is the best protection a buyer has.

The short version

Your deposit is serious money with strict rules. It must arrive on time, in the right form, payable to the right trust account, and it can only move when the contract, both signatures, or a court allows it. Understanding those rules before you write an offer removes most of the stress from the first week of a purchase.
